# Life and character in animated characters and robots: what makes motion feel alive versus merely correct (as of 2026)

Scope: principles, mechanisms and workflows that produce *life* and *personality* in game characters and physical robots, and how they are separated from the mechanisms that produce correctness, balance and adaptation. Written for a 14-joint, ~1.5–2 kg, arm-less bipedal "duck" with a 3-DoF neck/head (pitch/yaw/roll), i.e. a scaled-down BD-X. Each technique is tagged **[procedural]** (cheap, parametric, runs at runtime without an artist), **[authored]** (needs an animator and a clip/rig pipeline), **[learned/physical]** (needs physics, optimisation or RL), or **[embodiment]** (decided at mechanical design time). Fact and interpretation are kept apart: "Cited Findings" are sourced; "Inferences" are mine.

Research date: 2026-11-05. Note on sources: the two most-cited primary papers (Grandia et al. RSS 2024 on BD-X; Müller et al. 2025 on Olaf) were read in full via their arXiv HTML; the SIGGRAPH 2024 gait paper was read through its US patent, because the Disney PDF exceeded the fetch limit; Takayama et al. 2011 and Ribeiro & Paiva 2019 were read from the PDF text.

## Key question 1: Disney's 12 principles — how each is implemented in engines or robots, and what a rigid robot cannot do

### Takeaway
Nearly every principle survives on a rigid robot except squash-and-stretch and physically-driven follow-through/overlap; the robot-animation literature (Ribeiro & Paiva 2012/2019; van Breemen 2004) substitutes posture-based squash, timing scaling, arcs on the head, weight-shift asymmetry and keep-alive idles, and explicitly warns that inertial follow-through is both dangerous and balance-threatening on hardware. The one principle with controlled experimental evidence on a robot is anticipation ("forethought") plus reaction, which measurably raised appeal, approachability and perceived intelligence (Takayama, Dooley & Ju, HRI 2011).

### Cited Findings
- Thomas and Johnston's twelve Disney principles are the reference set; Ribeiro & Paiva summarise them with robot-relevant glosses, e.g. "Anticipation reveals the intentions of the character", "Arcs draw the trajectories of natural motions, making them feel less machine-like", and "Exaggeration makes some features more wild and relevant" — [Ribeiro & Paiva, "Nutty-based Robot Animation – Principles and Practices", arXiv 1904.02898 (2019)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898).
- Van Breemen's definition of robot animation: "The process of computing how the robot should act such that it is believable and interactive"; he showed Slow In/Out applied to the iCat under the name "Merging Logic" — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898).
- Ribeiro & Paiva re-define robot animation as "the workflow and processes that give a robot the ability of expressing identity, emotion and intention during autonomous interaction", and distinguish it from animatronics ("expressive motion for robots that can be faithfully played back") — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898).
- Squash and stretch on a rigid robot: "besides relying on the design, we can also create a squash and stretch effect by using poses and body movement … if the character is becoming shorter in height, it should become larger in length, and a humanoid robot can perform that by correctly bending its arms and legs" (demonstrated on NAO) — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898). **[authored, or procedural if parameterised as crouch-with-spread]**
- Anticipation on robots: "It is common for anticipation to be expressed by a shorter movement that reflects the opposite of the action"; a service robot "can incorporate anticipation to mark … that it is going to start to move, and in which direction" — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898). **[authored or procedural]**
- Arcs: "A typical robotic movement would make the head rotate only" horizontally; robots whose arms are rigid produce arcs "almost" automatically through their mechanics, so arcs matter most for the head/gaze and for "pre-animated motions" — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898). **[procedural: add a vertical dip to any yaw gaze shift]**
- Secondary action and idle ("keep-alive"): "A character should not stand stiff and still … Making them blink their eyes once and a while, or adding a soft, sinusoidal motion to the body to simulate breathing (lat. anima) contribute strongly to the illusion of life." Blink timing rule: blinks "often go unnoticed or may even disrupt the intended emotion" during a dramatic expression, so "perform them at the beginning or end of such expressions … blinking also works better if performed before and between gaze-shifts" — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898). **[procedural]**
- Solid drawing becomes Asymmetry: "a character should neither stand stiff and still, nor does it stand symmetrically. We generally put more weight in one leg than on the other, and shift the weight from one leg to the other … The only case in which we want symmetry is when we actually want to convey the feeling of stiffness" — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898). **[procedural offset over the balance controller, or authored]**
- Timing: "If the movement is too slow, the robot will seem like it is walking on the moon"; "Some studies have revealed a correlation between acceleration and perceived arousal"; scaling playback speed of the same clip changes the read from bored to engaged — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898). **[procedural: a playback-rate parameter]**
- Follow-through on robots is discouraged: "Follow-through might also cause a robot to loose balance … we consider that follow-through should better not be used in most robots", and it "is much more likely to be perceived by humans as dangerous, because it can give the impression that the robot slightly lost control over its body". Overlapping action "should be 'animated' by natural physics", i.e. "fur, hair or cloth on some parts of the robot", decided "at the robot design stage" — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898). **[embodiment]**
- Robot motion signals must be smoother than CG ones: "a motion signal generated for a robot should be at least C2 continuous"; their Nutty Motion Filter outputs "a C1, C2 or C3 signal … saturated by its velocity, acceleration and/or jerk limits" with character parameters for "smooth in/out, smooth damping, or if and how the motion should produce follow-through such as overshooting or controlled damped oscillation" — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898). **[procedural]**
- Appeal becomes "Expectation" ("Delivering on Expectations", after Wistort): "if it is a robotic dog, then it should bark and wag its tail. But if it is not able to do that, then maybe it should not be a dog" — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898).
- Takayama, Dooley (Pixar) & Ju, HRI 2011, N=273 online video study of a PR2: forethought was shown by "engagement" (translate forward/back a very small distance), "confidence" (torso lift "up and down a very small amount"; up = positive, down = negative) and timing: "it had to be designed to show expressive movement just an instant before it performed the functional task … It also required the robot to look in the direction it was about to turn. This adds time to the performance." — [Takayama et al. 2011 PDF](https://www.wendyju.com/publications/151r-takayama.pdf).
- Results: forethought made viewers more sure of their reading (M 5.69 vs 5.20, F(1,1067)=26.43, p<.0001), more appealing (4.83 vs 4.27, p<.001) and more approachable (5.05 vs 4.54, p<.001); showing a goal-oriented reaction raised perceived competence/intelligence (4.72 vs 3.86) "regardless of whether it completed the task successfully". Keyword-match readability did *not* improve ("varied more between tasks") — [Takayama et al. 2011](https://www.wendyju.com/publications/151r-takayama.pdf).
- Design implication stated by the authors: "the executive level processing of an autonomous robotic system can identify upcoming tasks and trigger a task-oriented expressive behavior that shows forethought immediately before performing a functional task" and "trigger reaction behaviors consistent with the success or failure" — [Takayama et al. 2011](https://www.wendyju.com/publications/151r-takayama.pdf). **[authored clips, procedurally triggered]**
- Game-side corroboration of timing/exaggeration craft (Overwatch, David Gibson, Animation Bootcamp): the team "broke rigs for snappier timing", used "2D-style smear frames to enhance movement arcs", "overshot extreme poses, held key poses longer than usual, and gave arms slightly different timing, since perfect symmetry looks stiff" — [Babel summary of Gibson's talk](https://babel.speldesign.uu.se/?p=113918) (secondary, student notes).

### Inferences
- Principle-by-principle verdict for a rigid 14-joint duck:
  - Squash & stretch: impossible as deformation; substitute = crouch/extend of the legs plus head-tuck (posture-based squash) **[authored, parameterisable]**; a foam/fabric costume gives real deformation **[embodiment]**.
  - Anticipation: fully available; the cheapest high-value ingredient (head turn toward the goal, small counter-motion before a step or a hop) **[procedural for gaze/lean; authored for clips]**.
  - Staging: on a robot means "one thing at a time" and staging the head toward the audience; cheap **[procedural]**.
  - Follow-through / overlapping: do not animate it into the legs (balance); put it in passive parts (antennae, tail feathers, costume) **[embodiment]**, or in a damped overshoot filter on the head only **[procedural]**.
  - Slow in/out and arcs: a motion filter plus "yaw gaze shifts get a vertical dip" rule **[procedural]**.
  - Secondary action and idle: breathing sinusoid, blink scheduler, weight-shift, occasional authored "idle breaks" **[procedural + small authored library]**.
  - Timing and exaggeration: expose playback rate and amplitude as parameters so one clip reads as several moods; exaggeration is capped by torque/velocity limits and by balance (see KQ4) **[procedural knobs on authored content]**.
  - Appeal / expectation: a duck should do duck things (waddle, head-bob, preen, shake); the character brief constrains the clip library **[authored]**.
- The HRI evidence says anticipation and reaction are not decoration: they changed perceived intelligence and appeal even when the robot failed. For a hobby robot that will fail often, a cheap "reaction to failure" clip is high leverage.

### Gaps
- Takayama 2011 was a video-prototype study; the authors note they could play Maya animations on a physical PR2 but had not tested live. I found no later controlled study of anticipation on a *balancing* legged robot.
- I could not get the full text of Ribeiro & Paiva's 2012 "The Illusion of Robotic Life" (only the 2019 Nutty paper, which restates and extends its principles).
- No source quantifies how much exaggeration (amplitude, speed) a given balance controller tolerates; this is only described qualitatively (KQ4).

## Key question 2: Game examples and techniques (idle variation, look-at, head-leads-body, breathing/sway layers, additive personality, springs, eye darts, reactions, attention systems, juice)

### Takeaway
Games get "life" from a stack of cheap runtime layers (attention/interest model → look-at IK → additive idle/breathing/sway → spring-driven secondary parts → reaction triggers) on top of a small set of hand-authored clips whose timing and asymmetry carry personality; the best-documented examples are Trico's interest-driven AI-first pipeline (CEDEC 2017), Overwatch's personality-first idles, Naughty Dog's additive sway plus neck/spine IK, and the "juice" talk's layering of feedback.

### Cited Findings
- **The Last Guardian (Trico), genDESIGN, CEDEC 2017 (Masanobu Tanaka):** per-frame order is "AI → animation → procedural animation → physics → fur → rendering"; the aim was to "Make the player feel that Trico has a mind"; Trico "doesn't talk, so he expresses emotion through animation alone" — [translated slide deck](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1cYDabTQGYe09w3PyUU4XLrDn2fHy9X5eJIFUjVDt89U/htmlpresent). **[procedural attention + authored clips]**
- Trico's attention system: "Every object or character has an interest value"; interest is "reduced ('digested') after Trico acts on it, rises over time, and is affected by distance"; "The highest value becomes the focus of attention" and attention "can shift to something behind Trico" — [CEDEC 2017 slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1cYDabTQGYe09w3PyUU4XLrDn2fHy9X5eJIFUjVDt89U/htmlpresent). **[procedural]**
- Trico's head/neck: a "Lion Dance Method" that "sets the head's position and rotation first, then solves the neck. Placing the rotation axis toward the front of the head increased expressiveness" — [CEDEC 2017 slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1cYDabTQGYe09w3PyUU4XLrDn2fHy9X5eJIFUjVDt89U/htmlpresent). Tanaka also said Trico "never uses fully automatic generation", the character "is designed with the idea of always adjusting the original animation" — [gameanim.com CEDEC summary](https://www.gameanim.com/?p=15474). **[procedural head-first solve over authored base]**
- Trico procedural legs: "a landing point is chosen when a foot lifts, then rechecked during the motion … Feet are pinned to prevent slipping, and posture adjusts based on foot displacement" — [CEDEC 2017 slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1cYDabTQGYe09w3PyUU4XLrDn2fHy9X5eJIFUjVDt89U/htmlpresent). **[procedural (games); on a robot this is the balance controller's job]**
- **Overwatch (David Gibson, Animation Bootcamp):** the idle "needs to convey their personality on top of their movement speed and function in-game"; Tracer's idle is a "wide stance, faces the camera head-on, leans back"; Mei's is "feet together, a slight imbalance, and her body angled away from the camera"; personality references (Zooey Deschanel, Rapunzel, Giselle) capture "posture, movement style, or personality traits" rather than exact motion — [Babel summary](https://babel.speldesign.uu.se/?p=113918). **[authored]**
- Overwatch first-person (Matthew Boehm, GDC 2017 "Animation Bootcamp: The First Person Perspective in Overwatch"): "the artistic and technical challenges of creating personality in first person animation while staying true to the designer's intent for gameplay", e.g. "How would McCree reload a revolver and can you do it in 45 frames?" — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024319/Animation-Bootcamp-The-First-Person); [Game Developer coverage](https://gamedeveloper.com/art/video-conveying-character-via-first-person-animations-in-i-overwatch-i-). **[authored under a hard timing budget]**
- **Naughty Dog, "Creating a Character in Uncharted: Drake's Fortune":** "Procedural IK on neck / spine joints" for look-at; partial animations "made the character look stiff", so they switched to additive animation "to keep the base motion while adding sway in the arms and head" — [Naughty Dog slides (SlideShare)](https://www2.slideshare.net/naughty_dog/creating-a-character-in-uncharted-drakes-fortune). **[procedural IK + additive layers on authored base]**
- **David Rosen (Wolfire), GDC 2014 Animation Bootcamp:** "how to use simple procedural techniques to achieve interactive and fluid animations using very few key frames" — [gameanim.com](https://www.gameanim.com/tag/procedural/). **[procedural]**
- A 2023 MIG paper on gaze notes that "look-at animations are generated procedurally in order to handle any desired target direction, however, this procedural animation can have a robotic quality" — [MIG 2023 via unpaywall](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F3623264.3624438). (I did not read the full paper; cited for the caveat.)
- Commercial tooling encodes the gaze ordering used in practice: an Unreal NPC plugin lists "the eyes lead, the head and neck follow, with natural limits" — [Idle Weaver listing](https://www.fab.com/listings/fae005c3-35e9-4800-8a36-662106af5a55). The classical animation source for "lead and follow" is overlapping action / Art Babbitt's "successive breaking of joints", taught in Richard Williams' *The Animator's Survival Kit* lesson "Head and Shoulders Don't Turn at the Same Time" — [Animation Apprentice blog](https://animationapprentice.blogspot.com/2020/05/head-and-shoulders-dont-turn-at-same.html). I found no source naming "eyes lead" as a formal Williams principle. **[procedural ordering rule]**
- **Secondary motion (springs/dynamic bones):** Unreal's AnimDynamics is the engine's spring-chain node; forum reports say it is hard to get "a wiry, metallic, springy, bouncy" antenna, it tends to assume parts "dangling down, as opposed to standing straight up like a strand of grass", and pawn acceleration must be fed in as wind — [Unreal forum: springy antenna](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/anim-dynamics-how-to-set-up-a-springy-antenna/68738); [more on AnimDynamics](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/more-information-on-animdynamics/54267). Spring-chain guidance from another engine: floppy ears/hair use low stiffness (0.1–0.25), low damping, a little gravity; "a very stiff chain follows the animation almost exactly" — [AzureLib secondary motion docs](https://moddedmc.wiki/en/project/azurelib/latest/docs/misc/animating/secondary_motion). **[procedural]**
- **"Juice it or lose it" (Martin Jonasson & Petri Purho, 2012):** a block-breaking game improved step by step by "adding particles, screenshake, sound, animations one layer at a time"; listed on the GDC Vault under GDC Europe 2012 and widely dated to Nordic Game May 2012 — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1016789/Juice-It-or-Lose); [gamejuice summary](https://gamejuice.pages.dev/articles/juice-it-or-lose-it-lessons). Sources disagree on venue. **[procedural feedback layers]**
- **Astro Bot (Team Asobi, GDC 2025 "The Making of ASTRO BOT", Nicolas Doucet):** an idle shown in press where Astro is "hopping up and down on his feet and pointing forward, almost like a little kid that's demanding you go to the playground"; the pitch promised "overflowing charm" tied to "art, animation, music" — [GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/platformer/astro-bot-went-through-23-pitch-iterations-before-its-director-promised-playstation-happy-gameplay-and-overflowing-charm-though-it-did-once-end-with-robot-decapitation-that-made-some-people-really-upset). **[authored idle break that also communicates intent to the player]**
- **Zelda: Breath of the Wild, GDC 2017 ("Change and Constant")**: Takizawa built a 2D prototype "to experiment with ideas on Link's actions" — [Destructoid](https://destructoid.com/?p=197094). The talk is about design process, not animation feel.
- Idle animations in general "serve to give games personality, as an Easter Egg for the player, or for maintaining realism" — [Idle animation (Wikipedia mirror)](https://wiki.krisyotam.com/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic_2026-03/Idle_animation); AnimSchool's idle breakdown (Seth Kendall) treats idles as adding "life and personality in subtle detail" — [AnimSchool blog](https://blog.animschool.edu/?p=709).

### Inferences
- The recurring game architecture is: (1) an *attention/interest* model picks a target **[procedural]**, (2) a *look-at* solver drives eyes→head→neck→torso in that order with per-stage lag and limits **[procedural]**, (3) *additive* idle layers (breathing, sway, weight shift) run under everything **[procedural]**, (4) *springs* animate passive appendages **[procedural]**, (5) short *authored* clips (idle breaks, reactions, emotes) are triggered by events **[authored + procedural trigger]**, (6) locomotion/contact is solved by IK/physics (games) or the balance controller (robots) **[learned/physical]**.
- Trico's "head position and rotation first, then solve the neck" is directly transferable to a duck with a 3-DoF neck: author or command the *head* pose (what the audience reads), and solve neck pitch/yaw/roll from it, rather than animating neck joints.
- Personality in games lives mostly in *authored* posture and timing (Overwatch's idles, asymmetric arm timing), not in the procedural layers; the procedural layers prevent "stiff" and "dead", the authored clips supply "who".
- Overwatch's "45 frames" and Astro's "idle that also points forward" show that life and function are co-designed: the budget comes from gameplay/balance, the character fills it.

### Gaps
- I did not find a Nintendo first-party talk on Link's or Mario's animation feel (the GDC 2018 "Animation Pipeline of Mario" entry is Ubisoft's *Mario + Rabbids*, not Nintendo); Nintendo's CEDEC 2018 material I found is Miyamoto's keynote. Nintendo "character feel" claims would rest on fan analyses, so I excluded them.
- The GDC 2017 Overwatch "idle" notes are a student summary of a bootcamp talk; the primary video was not fetched.
- No primary source for Unity Dynamic Bone specifics was found; only Unreal AnimDynamics forum material.

## Key question 3: Robots as characters — Disney (BD-X, stylized gaits, Olaf, Kiwi/Groot, hopper, gaze bust), Anki Cozmo/Vector, Boston Dynamics Spot, Kismet, Ameca, and the HRI "illusion of life" literature

### Takeaway
Disney's published pipeline is the clearest template: animators author in Maya on a rig that matches the robot's DoF, a procedural gait tool turns a few artist samples into feasible walks, RL policies imitate the references and keep balance, and an animation engine layers background idle, triggered clips and live joystick offsets at show time; Anki did the analogous thing on a 4-motor toy with Maya and a procedural face; Boston Dynamics exposes animation as a "request to the balancing controller". The HRI literature (Kismet, Hoffman & Ju, ELEGNT) adds that attention/gaze and task-timed expression are what make a robot read as having a mind, and that expression must not contradict the robot's actual capabilities.

### Cited Findings
**Disney BD-X (Grandia et al., "Design and Control of a Bipedal Robotic Character", RSS 2024, arXiv Jan 2025)**
- Robot: 15.4 kg, 0.66 m tall, "5 per leg and 4 for the neck and head" = 14 main actuated joints plus show functions (antennae, eye colour/radius, head lamp); knees bend backwards; ankle actuators at the feet "with no ankle roll"; "Rounded, urethane-foam-molded soles provide passive roll and impact damping"; developed "in less than a year" — [arXiv 2501.05204 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1); [RSS 2024 p103](https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss20/p103.html). **[embodiment]**
- Design loop: animators used "classical animation tools" with a rig of "links and spherical joints" to study proportions and range of motion; Maya for episodic motions; a procedural gait tool built walking cycles from rigid-body dynamics, whose joint positions/velocities/torques fed back into actuator selection and structure — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1).
- Animation engine, three layers: "(1) A looping background animation (eye blinks, antenna motion). (2) Operator-triggered clips from an artist library, blended in and out. Blend ramps last 0.1 s for show functions and 0.35 s for body motion. (3) Joystick-driven offsets to head and torso pose, or to walking velocities." — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1). **[authored clips + procedural blending + live puppetry]**
- Policy commands: standing = head height/orientation offsets plus torso height/orientation in a "path frame"; walking = head offsets plus 2D path velocity and turning rate; episodic = a monotonic phase signal, no operator input — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1).
- Policies: one standing, one walking, several episodic (happy dance, excited, jump, tantrum shown); PPO, 50 Hz policy, 600 Hz comms, 37.5 Hz low-pass; 100k iterations ≈ 22 days on an RTX 4090 but "about 1,500 iterations (~30 min) are enough to preview nominal behavior" — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1). **[learned]**
- Operator interface: Steam-Deck-style two-stick controller; left stick posture, right stick gaze "with the torso contributing when the neck reaches its limits"; face/shoulder buttons trigger "happy, angry, anxious, curious, yes, no, scan"; trackpads trigger eight episodic motions; velocity capped at 50 % unless R1 is held; "operators without robotics backgrounds were trained" — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1).
- Deployment: up to three robots at once, ~10 h of public shows without a fall; feedback that "the visible puppeteer reduces believability"; the system "does not plan long-term behavior" — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1).

**Disney stylized gait design (Hopkins, Wiedebach, Cesare, Bishop, Knoop, Bächer, "Interactive Design of Stylized Walking Gaits for Robotic Characters", ACM TOG / SIGGRAPH 2024; US patent 12,293,445)**
- Abstract: "an interactive, procedural animation technique that interfaces directly with a model-based control stack"; "Artists encode each walking style as a set of sample parameters which are translated into whole-body reference trajectories"; a "phase-space blending strategy … interpolates a set of example walk cycles authored by the animator while preserving contact constraints" — [Disney Research PDF listing](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/Interactive_Design_of_Stylized_Walking_Gaits_for_Robotic_Characters-5.pdf); [Disney Research page](https://la.disneyresearch.com/?p=27530). **[authored samples → procedural generalisation → model-based control]**
- What the animator edits (patent): three timing intervals (cycle, left swing, right swing) each with min/max; stance splay, width and X-bias (footholds are derived, avoiding "having to manually define the foothold poses"); cubic-spline function curves for sole translation/rotation in a swing frame, pelvis height and rotation, arm/spine/neck joint angles with "knot position and tangent controls". Horizontal pelvis translation is *not* animator-controlled: "A dynamic planner optimizes it to keep the center of pressure in the support polygon." The editor "warns the animator when double-support duration falls below a minimum threshold." — [US12293445B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12293445B2/en).
- Blending: nine samples (forward fast/slow, step in place, reverse fast/slow, strafe L/R, turn L/R), blended in parameter space "as opposed to the corresponding joint trajectories"; contact events are mapped to fixed phase values so "contact event times … will always map to the equivalent event time in another cycle" — [US12293445B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12293445B2/en).
- Stated rationale: "animation content that is authored for a robot must be governed by the laws of physics", and post-processing for hardware "may significantly alter the resulting gait, thereby losing the original artistic intent" — [US12293445B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12293445B2/en).

**Disney Olaf (Müller, Knoop, Mylonopoulos, Serifi, Hopkins, Grandia, Bächer, "Olaf: Bringing an Animated Character to Life in the Physical World", arXiv Dec 2025, v2 Apr 2026)**
- 88.7 cm, 14.9 kg, 25 DoF (6 per leg, 2 per shoulder, 3 neck, jaw, eyebrow, 4 eyes); legs are asymmetric under a "soft foam skirt" — [arXiv 2512.16705](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16705). **[embodiment: the costume supplies overlap/deformation]**
- "The animation rig has the same degrees of freedom as the robot's articulated backbone, so references map directly"; the gait tool (Hopkins et al. 2024) made "stylized walk cycles with heel-toe motion"; without heel-toe the gait looks "more robotic" (qualitative, video) — [arXiv 2512.16705](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16705).
- Split: RL controls legs, 3-DoF neck and torso; "Classical control handles the show functions: eyes (yaw, coupled pitch, eyelid), eyebrows, jaw, and arms. These have low inertia and are kept out of the policy." — [arXiv 2512.16705](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16705).
- Same three-layer Animation Engine as BD-X: background (eye saccades, arm adjustments), triggered clips (gestures, spoken lines), joystick (gaze/posture standing; gaze/velocity walking) — [arXiv 2512.16705](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16705).
- Believability is fragile: "small inconsistencies, such as rough foot impacts or jitter, can break the character's lifelike appearance"; a foot-impact reward "reduces the mean sound level by 13.5 dB" but "also smooths out subtle reference details, such as the mid-swing dip of the foot"; a thermal barrier reward keeps neck actuators under 80 °C (without it 100 °C in ~40 s), and "the policy learns to relax tracking near the temperature limit" — [arXiv 2512.16705](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16705). **[learned]**
- Tracking error on hardware: 3.87° ± 2.40° standing, 4.02° ± 2.01° walking; policy 50 Hz → 600 Hz first-order hold → 37.5 Hz low-pass — [arXiv 2512.16705](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16705).

**Disney AMOR (Alegre, Serifi, Grandia, Müller, Knoop, Bächer et al., SIGGRAPH 2025, arXiv 2505.23708)**
- One policy conditioned on reward weights "spanning the Pareto front"; "smoothness terms typically need to be weighted much higher for satisfactory results on real hardware"; "prioritizing smoothness conflicts with lower-body tracking"; on a 20-DoF biped, a double pirouette needed time-varying weights ("a high velocity weight … during the initial part", "a higher smoothing weight … towards the end"); tuning "took approximately 1 day" versus 5 days of training — [arXiv 2505.23708](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23708v1). **[learned, with post-hoc artistic knobs]**

**Disney Project Kiwi / Groot (2021) and gaze bust (IROS 2020)**

### Inferences
- The three Disney papers describe one consistent split (also used by Anki and Boston Dynamics): *what the audience reads* (head/neck pose, torso attitude, show functions, clip selection, timing) is authored/puppeteered and passed through as command signals; *what keeps it upright* (leg joints, pelvis horizontal translation, step timing under disturbance) is owned by the controller/policy, which imitates the author's reference only as far as physics allows.
- Low-inertia expressive parts (eyes, eyelids, antennae, a duck's bill or tail) should be open-loop/classical and kept out of the policy — Olaf states this explicitly, BD-X does it for show functions. For the duck: eyes/LEDs/antennae are the "free" expression channel.
- Disney's gaze bust and Kismet are two decades apart and have the same architecture: perception → saliency/curiosity score with habituation → discrete gaze states → low-level breathing/blink/saccade layer. This is the attention engine a duck needs, and it is entirely procedural.
- Anki's lesson is scale-independent: a 4-motor toy still used a film-grade Maya pipeline with live hardware preview, a procedural face, and an event→reaction→emotion loop. The 43-parameter procedural face is the Cozmo analogue of a duck's eye/head micro-motion layer.
- ELEGNT's negative results are the caution: expressive motion that contradicts capability reads as fake, and expression on purely functional tasks adds little. For the duck, expression should be strongest in social/idle contexts and lightest during precision tasks (e.g. stepping onto something).

## Key question 4: The split between "life" and "physics" — what the animator controls, what the controller controls, how expressive motion is layered over balance, and what hardware limits exaggeration, timing and anticipation

### Takeaway
In Disney's BD-X/Olaf stack the animator owns head/neck targets, torso attitude and height, show functions, clip timing and the *style* of the gait samples; the RL policy owns the leg joints (authored leg targets are literally "ignored because they are not policy inputs") and is pushed toward the author's style by imitation rewards weighted ~7× more on the neck than the legs, while regularisation, impact-noise and thermal rewards deliberately erode style where hardware demands it. Hardware caps exaggeration through torque/velocity saturation (BD-X's jump saturates knee and neck), through jitter and foot-impact noise that "break the character's lifelike appearance", and through blend/latency budgets (0.1–0.35 s ramps); anticipation costs time (Takayama) and follow-through costs balance (Ribeiro & Paiva).

### Cited Findings
- BD-X, authored vs. policy: "The neck and head targets come from the animation engine. The standing torso height and orientation also come from animation. Show functions … are driven directly by the animation output and are open-loop." "The leg joints are controlled by the learned policy … leg joint positions from the animation are ignored because they are not policy inputs. During walking, the lower body is determined entirely by the periodic policy and commanded path velocities." — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1).
- BD-X, how style is kept: imitation rewards track reference torso position/orientation/velocities, "joint positions (neck weight 100, leg weight 15) and foot-contact matching"; regularisation penalises "torque, acceleration, and action jerk"; episodic policies use "phase-windowed reward boosts for 'excited motion' and 'jump'"; "The paper does not describe a residual formulation" — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1).
- BD-X, hardware limits: peak torques 34 N·m (hip flex/add, knee), 24 N·m (hip rot, ankle, lower neck), 4.8 N·m (head); "Torque limits are reached during the jump, when the knee and neck actuators saturate"; tracking error 0.027–0.123 rad; walking max 0.7 m/s; "Each episodic motion needs its own policy, which the authors describe as costly to scale" — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1).
- BD-X, live layering: background + triggered clips + joystick "are layered and blended continuously"; blend ramps "0.1 s for show functions and 0.35 s for body motion"; gaze on the right stick "with the torso contributing when the neck reaches its limits"; a "motion stop" freezes joints "at high gains after 0.5 s" — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1).
- Olaf, same split plus classical show functions: RL for legs/neck/torso; "Classical control handles the show functions: eyes … eyebrows, jaw, and arms. These have low inertia and are kept out of the policy"; policies conditioned on "neck orientation, torso orientation, and height" (standing) or "neck orientation and path velocity" (walking), with control inputs randomised in training; "Joint weights differ between neck and legs because of their very different reflected inertia" — [arXiv 2512.16705](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16705).
- Olaf, physics eroding style on purpose: impact reward "reduces the mean sound level by 13.5 dB" but "smooths out subtle reference details, such as the mid-swing dip of the foot"; thermal reward makes the policy "relax tracking near the temperature limit"; "small inconsistencies, such as rough foot impacts or jitter, can break the character's lifelike appearance" — [arXiv 2512.16705](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16705).
- Gait editor, what is withheld from the artist: pelvis horizontal translation, which "a dynamic planner optimizes … to keep the center of pressure in the support polygon"; CoP inside the support polygon is "a necessary condition for dynamic feasibility"; the editor warns on too-short double support — [US12293445B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12293445B2/en).
- AMOR: "Precisely tracking more dynamic reference motions, which are generally less feasible, introduces jitter"; on hardware "the real robot exhibits significantly higher jitter in the joint positions and velocities" than in sim; raising the smoothness weight reduced it; dynamic motions needed time-varying weights — [arXiv 2505.23708](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23708v1).
- Spot: "prioritizing balance first and then following the specified steps"; a typical robot "will adhere strictly to the directive—even if it falls", unlike a human dancer; animation "is still treated as a request to the balancing controller"; the parametric workflow does not let users "specify exact joint angles or torques" — [In Step with Spot](https://bostondynamics.com/blog/in-step-with-spot/).
- Timing cost of anticipation: showing thought "adds time to the performance, but allows the robot to appear as though it is thinking about the task before it performs it" — [Takayama et al. 2011](https://www.wendyju.com/publications/151r-takayama.pdf).
- Balance cost of follow-through: "Follow-through might also cause a robot to loose balance … Many robot systems actually will try to defend themselves against the follow-through caused by its own movements" — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898).
- Expression that exceeds capability backfires: participants found expressive movement "less predictable and less steady" on a precision task and "fake" when it conflicted with capabilities; "there needs to be a balance" between engaging motion and task speed — [ELEGNT, arXiv 2501.12493](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12493).
- Trico (game-side analogue): hand animation is the base and procedural systems adjust it ("always adjusting the original animation"); legs re-plan footholds during the step and feet are pinned — [gameanim.com](https://www.gameanim.com/?p=15474); [CEDEC slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1cYDabTQGYe09w3PyUU4XLrDn2fHy9X5eJIFUjVDt89U/htmlpresent).

### Inferences
- Concrete ownership map, as published by Disney and transferable one-to-one to a 14-joint duck (BD-X is also 5+5+4 = 14):
  - Animator/puppeteer owns: head height and 3-axis orientation, torso pitch/roll/yaw and height (standing), path velocity and turn rate (walking), clip choice and trigger timing, show functions (eyes, antennae, lights), the *shape* of the gait samples (stance, swing curves, pelvis bob, timing intervals).
  - Controller/policy owns: all leg joints, horizontal pelvis motion/CoP, contact timing under disturbance, torque/velocity/thermal/jitter trade-offs.
  - The interface between them is a small command vector (BD-X: head offsets + torso pose or velocity + phase), not joint targets. Authored leg motion only enters through the imitation reward at training time.
- "Style survives through the reward, not the command." If the duck's walk should waddle, the waddle has to be in the reference clips the policy imitates (as Olaf's heel-toe is); at runtime the operator cannot add it. The corollary is that every new *gait* style is a retrain (or an AMOR-style weight knob), whereas every new *head/torso* expression is free.
- Episodic whole-body stunts (jump, tantrum) are the expensive tier: one policy per clip, torque saturation, and a phase signal with no live input. For a hobby duck, keep such clips few and short, and preview them at ~1,500 iterations before committing to a full training run (BD-X's 30-minute preview rule).
- The hardware caps on exaggeration are: (a) actuator torque/velocity (saturation in BD-X's jump; 4.8 N·m head actuators), (b) reflected inertia of the head versus legs (hence different reward weights), (c) jitter and impact noise, which the audience reads as "not alive" (Olaf), and (d) thermal limits on a heavy head on a slim neck (Olaf). For a 1.5–2 kg duck with hobby servos, (a) and (c) will dominate; (d) matters if the head is proportionally heavy.
- Timing limits: blend ramps of 0.1 s (show) and 0.35 s (body) are Disney's practical floor for believable transitions on a biped; anticipation must be budgeted into every action's duration; follow-through should be confined to the head (damped overshoot filter) and passive parts.

### Gaps
- No source gives the maximum head/torso offset amplitude or rate the BD-X standing policy tolerates before balance degrades; the papers randomise control inputs in training but do not publish the ranges.
- Whether Disney blends authored *leg* clips into the policy at runtime (e.g. a kick) is not described; episodic motions appear to be entire policies.
- No quantitative study of how much style loss (e.g. the removed mid-swing dip) audiences notice.

## Key question 5: Workflow — how animators author for robots (Maya/Blender rigs matching the robot, playback through a controller, retargeting, animation-in-the-loop), including Blender pipelines for hobby robots

### Takeaway
The working pattern across Disney, Anki, Boston Dynamics and Willow Garage is: a DCC rig with exactly the robot's DoF, authored clips exported as joint keyframes or gait parameters, a fast preview loop (live motor sync at Anki; ~30-minute policy preview at Disney; "test on hardware first" at Boston Dynamics), and a runtime engine that blends clips and live inputs. For hobby robots the same pattern exists in Blender via the Servo Animation add-on (per-bone servo export, live streaming) and, for RL ducks, via the Open Duck reference-motion generator, which produces the imitation references the policy learns from.

### Cited Findings
- Disney BD-X: animators used a rig of "links and spherical joints" to explore proportions and range of motion, "Autodesk Maya generates the episodic motions", and the procedural gait tool produced walk cycles; the tools fed "joint positions, velocities, and torques" back into actuator selection — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1).
- Disney Olaf: "The animation rig has the same degrees of freedom as the robot's articulated backbone, so references map directly to hardware" — [arXiv 2512.16705](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16705).
- Disney gait editor: the artist edits timing intervals, stance attributes and spline function curves; the system derives footholds and pelvis horizontal motion; "an animator-driven workflow, with the animator keeping creative control while the engine enforces constraints" — [US12293445B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12293445B2/en).
- Iteration speed at Disney: a full policy takes ~22 days, but "about 1,500 iterations (~30 min) are enough to preview nominal behavior" — [arXiv 2501.05204](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1); AMOR lets "users … set the weights after training and directly observe the adapted behavior" — [arXiv 2505.23708](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23708v1).
- Anki: software "inside of Maya" so animators animate "an actual robot"; motors synced to Maya so animators "see movements on Cozmo prototypes in real time"; the GDC talk covered "Tools for fast animator iteration", "Scripts for testing and debugging", "Streaming audio and animation data to the robot" and "When programmatic corrections were needed" — [Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/cozmo-ai-robot-brings-wall-e-to-life/); [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024221/Cozmo-Animation-Pipeline-for-a).
- Boston Dynamics: animations authored in Maya or "hand-written as human-readable text files", parsed to an `Animation` protobuf, uploaded and referenced by name; "Keyframes can be dense or sparse"; robot logs of joint state, foot contacts and body pose "can serve directly as keyframes" (i.e. record-and-replay); Kathleen Brandes: "we need to make it work on hardware first" — [Spot SDK animations](https://dev.bostondynamics.com/docs/concepts/choreography/animations_in_choreographer); [In Step with Spot](https://bostondynamics.com/blog/in-step-with-spot/).
- Willow Garage/Pixar: PR2 URDF "converted into a Collada file" and imported into the animation suite; "not all of the kinematic constraints were maintained, particularly the speed of the torso lift"; they could "base physical robot behavior on animations from Maya on the PR2" but it lacked collision avoidance — [Takayama et al. 2011](https://www.wendyju.com/publications/151r-takayama.pdf).
- Hoffman & Ju's prototyping ladder: "character animation sketches, video prototyping, interactive movement explorations, Wizard of Oz studies, and skeletal prototypes" — [EPFL abstract](https://memento.epfl.ch/event/designing-robots-with-movement-in-mind).
- Ribeiro & Paiva's engine model: an animation engine takes inputs such as "gaze-target coordinates; expressive … commands such as reset posture, or activate idle-motion", composes "sequences, layers and stages" of animation blocks with blending operations, and post-filters all DoFs (Nutty Tracks is their "Level 3 programmable animation engine"; the Nutty Motion Filter has an online simulator) — [Ribeiro & Paiva 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.02898).
- Blender hobby pipeline: the Servo Animation add-on exports an armature's animation as servo positions, "each bone is treated as one servo", to an Arduino header or JSON, with min/max based on pulse lengths (PCA9685-style), a "live mode for streaming positions to a microcontroller in real time" and calibration; v2.1.1 (Jan 2026) requires Blender 4.2 LTS or newer — [GitHub timhendriks93/blender-servo-animation](https://github.com/timhendriks93/blender-servo-animation); [Blender Extensions](https://extensions.blender.org/add-ons/servo-animation/). Hackaday's walkthrough: build an armature matching the robot, rig, keyframe, export; BVH is an alternative; "a second clip must begin where the previous one ended" — [Hackaday 2022](https://hackaday.com/2022/05/11/animate-your-robot-in-blender).
- Open Duck Mini (apirrone): ~35 cm BD-X-style biped; policy trained in Isaac Gym (and a MuJoCo Playground variant); runtime on a Raspberry Pi Zero 2W; the separate "Open Duck reference motion generator" (`auto_waddle.py --duck open_duck_mini_v2`, with `--sweep`, a replay script and a gait playground) produces the imitation references, and its README lists a planned "format spec for converting some mocap data to use for the imitation reward" — [Open Duck Mini (ecosyste.ms mirror)](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fapirrone%2FOpen_Duck_Mini); [reference motion generator (Gitee mirror)](https://gitee.com/ncnynl/Open_Duck_reference_motion_generator). No Blender step was found in either repo.
- Open Duck Mini head commands: a third-party PS4 script sends `[vx, vy, yaw, neck_pitch, head_pitch, head_yaw, head_roll]` to the policy, with limits neck pitch [-0.34, 1.1], head pitch [-0.78, 0.3], head yaw [-0.5, 0.5] rad; the official runtime README marks keyboard head control "very experimental … it can break your duck's head" — [ps4_controller.py (Gitee mirror)](https://gitee.com/ncnynl/mcp-openduck/blob/master/ps4_controller.py); [Open Duck Mini Runtime README mirror](https://github.gg22.workers.dev/tunechr/Open_Duck_Mini_Runtime). Ranges differ between sources.

### Inferences
- The minimum viable "Disney-shaped" pipeline for a hobby duck is: (1) a Blender armature whose bones are exactly the 14 joints plus show-function bones, (2) authored clips exported as per-joint keyframes (Servo Animation JSON or a custom exporter), (3) a tiny runtime animation engine with three layers (background idle / triggered clip / live offsets) that emits only the policy's command vector (head pose, torso pose or velocity) plus open-loop show-function values, (4) the walking/standing policy trained on reference clips that carry the gait style. This matches BD-X/Olaf, Anki, and the Open Duck runtime's existing command vector.
- Record-and-replay (Boston Dynamics' logs-as-keyframes) is a cheap way to capture puppeteered performances on the duck into clips.
- "Animation-in-the-loop" at Anki meant live motor sync from Maya; the Blender add-on's live mode gives the same for head/show functions, but leg clips cannot be previewed live on a balancing robot without the policy, so leg style must be previewed in sim (Disney's 30-minute preview).
- Takayama's note that the Maya rig did not respect torso speed limits is the classic failure: enforce joint velocity/acceleration limits in the exporter or a C2 motion filter so authored clips are feasible before they reach hardware.

### Gaps
- No public source documents a Blender→RL-reference pipeline for the Open Duck (its references come from a procedural gait generator, not DCC clips); whether Open Duck v2's head commands are tracked by the policy or passed through as servo targets is unclear from the mirrors found.
- Anki's Maya plug-in, the exact streaming format and the "programmatic corrections" (e.g. for motor limits) are undocumented; the GDC video is paywalled.
- Disney's Maya rig conventions (how show functions and gait parameters are represented in the rig) are not described in the papers.

## Lessons for a layered robot controller

### Takeaway
Treat the duck as four stacked owners — embodiment, controller, procedural life layer, authored character — with a thin command vector between them, exactly as BD-X/Olaf do; buy "alive" with the cheap procedural layer, buy "who" with a small authored library, and buy "doesn't fall" with physics/learning.

### Inferences
- **Command interface, not joint targets.** The expression stack should emit only what BD-X's policy consumes: head pose offsets (3 axes + height), torso pitch/roll/yaw/height when standing, planar velocity + turn rate when walking, a phase for scripted stunts, plus open-loop show-function values. Leg joints are the policy's alone.
- **Put gait *style* in the training references** (waddle, head-bob, heel-toe analogue), with neck-vs-leg imitation weights reflecting inertia (BD-X 100 vs 15), and accept that smoothness/impact/thermal rewards will sand off fine detail; use AMOR-style weight conditioning if you want to tune style after training.
- **Cheap procedural "alive" layer, always on:** breathing sinusoid on torso height/pitch, blink scheduler (blink before/between gaze shifts, not mid-expression), eye saccades/darts, slow weight-shift asymmetry, head micro-motion, spring-simulated antennae/tail, and a C2 motion filter with slow-in/out and head-only damped overshoot. This is Disney's "background animation" layer and Ribeiro & Paiva's keep-alive.
- **Attention engine (procedural):** saliency/curiosity score per target (motion, faces, sound), habituation/decay, discrete states (idle/glance/engage/acknowledge), and an eyes→head→neck→torso look-at ordering with per-stage lag and limits; torso joins only when the neck saturates (BD-X's joystick rule).
- **Anticipation and reaction as triggers:** before any locomotion or stunt, insert a short "forethought" (look toward the goal, small counter-lean, pause); after any task, play a success/failure reaction. The HRI evidence says this raises appeal and perceived intelligence even on failure, and it costs only time.
- **Authored library, small and asymmetric:** a handful of personality idles/idle-breaks and emotes (BD-X ships ~7 triggered clips + ~8 episodic stunts), authored on a Blender rig with exactly the robot's DoF, exported as keyframes with velocity/acceleration limits enforced, blended with ~0.1 s (show) / ~0.35 s (body) ramps; playback-rate and amplitude knobs turn one clip into several moods.
- **Expensive tier, use sparingly:** whole-body episodic stunts (jump, tantrum, dance) need their own policy each and hit torque limits; preview in sim for ~30 minutes before a full run.
- **Embodiment does the overlap:** no follow-through in the legs; put it in passive feathers/antennae/costume and rounded compliant feet (BD-X's foam soles, Olaf's skirt).
- **Guardrails from the literature:** jitter and loud foot impacts kill the illusion faster than missing style; expression must never claim a capability the robot lacks (no "listening" tilt if there is no microphone); keep expression light during precision tasks and heavy in social/idle moments; a visible puppeteer reduces believability, so hide or automate the operator.
